\begin{abstract}
Can the United States continue borrowing to finance primary deficits and rising interest
expense indefinitely, or does the interaction eventually force a change in regime?  This
paper asks that question with a family of quarterly scenarios produced by the same Treasury
debt engine.  The main stress scenarios begin in 2026Q4 with a 500-basis-point premium on new
Treasury issuance for ten years and a 200-basis-point private-credit spread for two years.
Existing fixed-rate debt
retains its coupon until maturity.  New interest expense is allocated to domestic private,
foreign, and Federal Reserve holders; a configurable share enters demand; inflation affects
the Federal Reserve policy rule; and policy rates feed back into subsequent Treasury
interest.

An artificial control sets the spending of stress-created interest to zero, isolating the
Treasury refinancing arithmetic.  Mandatory quarterly clearing then keeps inflation near 2
percent while debt held by the public reaches 252 percent of GDP in 2056.  The main case
combines official 2026 holder shares with illustrative spending propensities: domestic private
recipients spend 25 percent of incremental interest on domestic output and foreign recipients
spend 5 percent.  Together, those assumptions send 15.67 percent of incremental cash interest
into domestic demand.  A grid of Federal Reserve responses keeps inflation below a
4-percent early-warning line through 2056 only when the effective spending share stays below
10 percent.  At the main-case 15.67-percent share, inflation remains above 4 percent beginning
in 2037Q3, passes 6 percent in 2048, and reaches 8.04 percent in 2056 while still rising.  A
published debt--yield sensitivity accelerates the path: inflation remains above 8 percent
beginning in 2051Q3, above 10 percent beginning in 2055Q1, and reaches 11.26 percent in 2056
alongside a 19.92-percent policy rate.

An illustrative, unestimated aggregate financing-capacity rule supplies the second test.  Under its central
sensitivity, required issuance exceeds 1.5 times reference capacity in 2040Q3, or 2039Q3
when debt-dependent yields are active.  A tight 1.25-times ceiling binds in 2034Q3; a wide
2.0-times ceiling remains slack through 2056.  The 4-percent date is the first warning in an
inflation path that continues to accelerate.  The financing-capacity date is a hard model
stop: automatic private placement has ended and a new fiscal, monetary, regulatory, or
payment regime must begin.

The paper then gives that break a conditional recovery.  The central private-clearing path
ends 2039Q2 with inflation at 4.89 percent, debt at 178.82 percent of GDP, annualized interest
expense at 20.49 percent of GDP, and the Fed's short-rate setting at 8.72 percent.  Beginning
in 2039Q3, a joint settlement is assumed to lower marginal Treasury rates gradually, permit
inflation to peak at 5.2 percent, and cause one year of negative 1-percent real growth.  The
cohort solver finds that a permanent primary-balance improvement of 5.48 percent of GDP,
phased in over four years, returns debt/GDP to 178.82 percent by 2056 and leaves it falling.
Debt first peaks at 209.27 percent in 2043; interest expense declines to 8.88 percent of GDP
and inflation to 2.5 percent by 2056.  The same favorable growth, inflation, and rate paths
without the fiscal package end at 265.14 percent debt/GDP.  The recovery's macro and yield
paths are declared assumptions; the required fiscal adjustment and Treasury cash flows are
the computed results.  In this conditional history, financing stops at the supplied
boundary and an assumed settlement replaces the later double-digit-inflation path
with a conditional recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{What would make federal borrowing break?}

The parable in Peter and Andrew Schiff's \emph{How an Economy Grows and Why It Crashes}
ends with foreigners acquiring claims on domestic wealth \cite{schiff}.  Ray Dalio describes
debt cycles that culminate in monetization, inflation, domestic conflict, reserve-currency
decline, and geopolitical change \cite{dalioChangingOrder,dalioDebtCycle}.  Those stories ask
the question studied here: if the federal government repeatedly borrows to cover the primary
deficit---the gap between noninterest spending and revenue---and an ever-larger interest bill,
what eventually forces the process to change?

\paragraph{One model, several scenarios.}
This paper investigates that question with a quarterly computer model of U.S. Treasury debt.
The model begins with a reconstructed 2026 stock of bills, notes, bonds, TIPS, and floating-
rate notes.  In every quarter it calculates coupon and principal payments, refinances maturing
securities, and issues additional debt for the primary deficit and cash interest.  It then
connects that Treasury ledger to a simple economic loop: some interest payments become
spending; spending affects output and inflation; the Federal Reserve raises rates in response;
and those rates increase the cost of the next round of Treasury borrowing.

The paper runs several scenarios through that common model.  The main stress scenarios add
five percentage points to new Treasury yields from 2026Q4 through 2036Q3 and add two
percentage points to private-credit rates for the first two years.  A no-shock comparison, an
artificial zero-spending control, alternative bondholder-spending shares, a grid of Federal
Reserve reactions, debt-dependent Treasury yields, and three buyer-capacity curves isolate
different parts of the mechanism.  Each reported date belongs to one of these stated
conditional scenarios.

\paragraph{What can stop automatic rollover?}
The Treasury ledger by itself can run forever.  If every auction is guaranteed to clear and
the government may issue whatever amount balances its accounts, each quarter's financing need
simply becomes debt carried into the next quarter.  Debt and interest can reach extraordinary
levels while the program continues producing another number.  A meaningful breaking point
therefore requires an economic limit outside the accounting identity.

The first diagnostic tracks inflation control under the initiating stress.  Inflation moves
freely while a Federal Reserve reaction rule raises rates in an attempt to return it toward
the reference path near 2 percent.  For reporting, the paper marks the start of the first run
lasting at least four consecutive quarters above 4 percent as an early warning that the rule
is failing to pull inflation back.
The economic severity comes from the subsequent path: whether inflation retreats or continues
through 6, 8, and 10 percent while policy rates rise with it.

The second diagnostic imposes a hard financing limit.  Gross issuance in a quarter
must cover maturing principal as well as the primary deficit and interest.  The financing-
capacity rule begins with the amount buyers absorb along the reference path.  Higher yields
attract additional buyers until a specified maximum multiple of that reference amount is
reached.  In the central curve, each 100-basis-point yield increase adds capacity equal to 5
percent of reference issuance, up to a total capacity of 1.5 times the reference amount.  When
required issuance exceeds that maximum, the simulation stops: the next quarter requires
Federal Reserve purchases, fiscal adjustment, compulsory absorption, inflationary
accommodation, or a change to promised payments.

\paragraph{What the scenarios show.}
The central macro result is a progressive loss of inflation control.  An artificial control that prevents
stress-created interest from entering demand produces much more debt but little inflation.
In the main case, domestic private recipients spend 25 percent of incremental interest on
domestic output and foreign recipients spend 5 percent.  Weighting those rates by official
holder shares sends 15.67 percent of incremental cash interest into domestic demand.  That
share lies above the model's 9.5-to-10-percent early-warning threshold.  Inflation remains
above 4 percent beginning in 2037Q3, above 6 percent beginning in 2048Q1, and reaches 8.04
percent in 2056.  Raising rates eventually adds enough Treasury interest to reinforce the
demand pressure, and every Federal Reserve rule tested at the main spending share ends with
inflation pressure still rising.  Adding debt-dependent yields produces sustained inflation
above 8 percent in 2051Q3 and above 10 percent in 2055Q1.

The separate financing-capacity experiment supplies the hard break.  Its central curve stops
the run in 2039Q3 when debt-dependent yields are active; tighter and wider curves
move that date from 2034Q3 to beyond 2056.  The experiments illustrate mechanisms
that could interrupt automatic debt rollover. Neither their dates nor an inevitable
breakdown are established independently of the spending and buyer-capacity assumptions.  CBO similarly emphasizes
that the timing of a U.S. fiscal crisis depends on more than debt/GDP alone \cite{cboCrisis}.

The recovery experiment asks what happens if Washington acts at that break instead of
continuing the unchanged-policy path.  It gives the economy a short recession, holds
inflation between 2.5 and 5.2 percent, and lets newly issued Treasury rates decline over
several years as the settlement gains credibility.  Under those stated conditions, the
cohort engine requires a permanent 5.48-percent-of-GDP primary-balance improvement phased in
over four years.  Debt rises for another four years because high-coupon securities remain in
the stock, peaks at 209 percent of GDP, and then declines to 179 percent in 2056.  The result
still leaves an unprecedented debt burden and a transformed federal budget.  It also shows
the size and duration of the adjustment that prevents the crisis from becoming the country's
permanent economic regime.

\section{Starting point and initiating stress}

The experiment starts in \textbf{2026Q4}.  Opening debt held by the public is approximately
\$32.2 trillion, or 99.2 percent of GDP.  The cohort ledger tracks bills, fixed-rate notes
and bonds, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), and floating-rate notes (FRNs) by
principal, coupon, and maturity.  It also carries a coarse nonmarketable public-debt
category.  The fiscal and economic reference paths come from CBO's February 2026 ten-year
baseline and long-term extension \cite{cbo2026,cboLongTerm}.

The initial stress adds five percentage points to each new-issuance rate for forty quarters.
In 2026Q4, the bill rate therefore rises from 3.278 to 8.278 percent, the note and
bond rate from 4.197 to 9.197 percent, and the TIPS real rate from 1.750 to 6.750 percent.
FRNs reset from the higher bill reference rate plus their fixed spreads.  Existing notes and
bonds keep their contractual coupons until maturity.  The premium disappears after 2036Q3;
subsequent rate movements come from the policy and debt-yield rules.

A 200-basis-point private-credit spread applies for the first eight quarters and enters the
output equation directly as a temporary recessionary impulse.  A structural
Treasury-to-mortgage pass-through lies outside the experiment.
The 500-basis-point Treasury premium is also a scenario trigger.  It represents an outer-
tail repricing large enough to test the system.  Its probability is unspecified, and the
experiment treats it as a sensitivity rather than a 2026 auction forecast.

These rules govern the unresolved stress path.  The recovery experiment changes course in
2039Q3.  It publishes a post-settlement path for growth, inflation, and marginal Treasury
rates, then solves for the primary-balance improvement required to return debt/GDP to its
2039Q2 level by 2056 and leave the ratio falling.  That terminal condition is explicit; it
belongs only to the recovery solver, while the unresolved stress scenarios remain untargeted.

The initiating rate shock reaches federal cash expense gradually.  Bills roll quickly and
FRNs reset; fixed-rate securities reprice only as they mature.  Each quarter the model
indexes TIPS, accrues interest, matures cohorts, refinances principal, and issues additional
debt for the primary deficit and cash interest.  Securities issued at quarter-end begin
adding interest in the next quarter.  This cohort structure is the accounting kernel shared
by every experiment below.

\section{How the stress unfolds: a hypothetical chronology}

Markets never label a move an ``exogenous shock.''  Investors, politicians, and television
anchors attach a story to it, even when they disagree about the underlying cause.  The
datelines and news bulletins below construct a hypothetical public history around a
crisis and a recovery. They are invented scenario events, not forecasts.  Through 2039Q2, bondholders spend 15.67 percent of their extra interest income on
domestic output, a widening debt/GDP gap raises longer-term yields, and the central buyer
curve requires progressively larger auction spreads.  The curve reaches its limit in
2039Q3.  Washington then changes course: the Fed bridges the market, Congress enacts a
four-year fiscal package, and marginal Treasury rates decline as the package gains
credibility.

The recovery is conditional in a precise way.  Its quarterly paths for inflation, real
growth, and new Treasury rates are published assumptions.  The cohort engine determines how
the inherited securities refinance, how much interest they generate, and how debt evolves.
It also solves for the permanent primary-balance improvement required to return debt/GDP to
its pre-settlement level by 2056 with the ratio falling.  The people, legislation, market
reactions, and quotations in the chronology are invented.  The numerical statements before
the settlement come from the sequential crisis run; those after it come from the conditional
recovery run.

In this history, the panic begins when budget negotiations collapse just before Treasury
exhausts its borrowing authority.  Moody's, which lowered the United States from Aaa to Aa1
in May 2025, announces a second one-notch cut, from Aa1 to Aa2 \cite{moodysUS}.  A poorly
received Treasury auction then leaves dealers holding far more securities than planned.
The stress test fixes the combined repricing at five percentage points.  The downgrade, legal
threat, and auction seizure supply the public explanation for an outer-tail move of that
imposed size: investors suddenly demand compensation for payment delay, inflation, and the
danger that a seized-up market will leave them unable to resell Treasuries.

\paragraph{Wednesday, October 14, 2026---the break.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Breaking News, 4:18 p.m.: A Treasury auction has cleared only after the largest one-day
yield increase in modern market history.  The selloff follows the collapse of congressional
budget talks and Moody's downgrade of the United States from Aa1 to Aa2.  The White House
says every federal payment will be honored.  Banks are repricing mortgages and corporate
credit tonight.
\end{quote}

Within days, buyers demand five additional percentage points to hold newly issued Treasury
debt.  The bill rate jumps from 3.278 to 8.278 percent; note and bond rates jump from 4.197 to
9.197 percent.  Private-credit rates rise by two percentage points as banks and bond investors
reprice newly quoted dollar loans.  Stocks sell off because Treasury yields form the base
discount rate used to value future corporate earnings: adding five percentage points lowers
the present value of those earnings, while dearer private credit points toward less
investment and lower profits.  Existing bonds also fall in market value, imposing immediate
losses on banks, funds, and leveraged holders.  Lenders pull mortgage rate sheets while they
reprice, and nightly news panels debate whether the United States is insolvent or merely
politically incapable of governing itself.  These asset-price reactions belong to the
invented news history; the macro equation receives only the stated two-point private-credit
spread.

The federal interest bill moves slowly.  Old notes and bonds still carry their contractual
coupons, so bills, floating-rate notes, and the first newly issued securities take the blow.
Inflation ends December at 2.37 percent and debt held by the public at 99.65 percent of GDP.
To most households, this still looks like a violent financial-market event rather than a
federal budget emergency.

\paragraph{Thursday, December 12, 2030---the headlines declare victory too soon.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Evening News: Inflation is back near 2 percent and private borrowing has begun to recover.
Administration officials say the economy has absorbed the Treasury shock.  A quieter number
in today's budget report shows federal interest expense above 8 percent of GDP.
\end{quote}

Bills have rolled repeatedly, and progressively larger cohorts of notes and bonds have
matured.  Treasury replaces them at crisis rates while also borrowing to cover the primary
deficit and the larger interest bill.  Debt reaches 116.28 percent of GDP.  During this
quarter, the higher-rate debt generates \$420 billion more cash interest than the economy
would have seen without the panic.  Bondholders spend \$65.9 billion of that additional
income on domestic output.  Interest expense reaches 8.36 percent of annualized GDP while
inflation is still only 2.21 percent.  The widening debt gap has also added 33 basis points
to the long-bond rate.  The apparent calm conceals a much more expensive debt stock.

\paragraph{Thursday, September 18, 2036---the feedback becomes visible.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Market Close: Treasury bill rates have reached 11.75 percent after the Federal Reserve's
latest increase.  Inflation is 3.77 percent.  Lawmakers are demanding lower rates while Fed
officials warn that cutting now would reignite price growth.
\end{quote}

The private-credit recession has faded, but the federal interest transfer is now supporting
demand.  The Fed's short-rate setting has reached 6.75 percent.  The original five-point crisis
premium still sits on top, producing an 11.75-percent bill rate.  The debt--yield response
adds another 1.37 percentage points to the long-bond rate, which reaches 12.57 percent.  Each
Fed increase depresses homebuilding, business investment, and consumer credit; it also raises
the coupon Treasury promises when the next security is sold.

Debt has reached 154.83 percent of GDP.  Interest expense absorbs 15.99 percent of annualized
GDP and the total deficit is 17.06 percent of GDP.  During this quarter alone, stress-created
interest sends another \$222 billion into domestic demand.  The central bank is pressing the
brake while the Treasury interest bill is pressing the accelerator.

\paragraph{Wednesday, October 1, 2036---Washington removes the panic premium.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Breaking News: Congress has replaced recurring debt-limit votes with permanent borrowing
authority for obligations already enacted.  The Federal Reserve has announced a standing
Treasury-market facility intended to prevent another auction seizure.  Treasury yields fall,
but dealers warn that the volume of securities still coming to market is overwhelming
traditional buyers.
\end{quote}

This is why the extra five percentage points disappear in this history.  Congress removes
the recurring legal threat to payment, and the Fed reduces the risk that dealers will be
left without market liquidity.  The five-point premium vanishes, but a 3.70-point auction-
clearing spread takes its place as Treasury asks private buyers to absorb ever more paper.
The bill rate therefore falls only from 11.75 to 10.62 percent.  Washington has repaired the
original panic while leaving the primary deficit and ten years of high-coupon debt in place.
Inflation risk and financing pressure survive the rescue.

\paragraph{Thursday, September 17, 2037---the Fed raises rates again.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Fed Raises Benchmark Again: With inflation moving above 4 percent, the central bank has
lifted its short-rate setting to 7.41 percent.  Officials say high real borrowing costs will
cool demand, but acknowledge that rising Treasury interest payments are weakening the effect.
\end{quote}

The Fed has continued tightening after the panic premium vanished: its short-rate setting
reaches 7.41 percent one year later.  Its reaction rule
responds to inflation with gradual quarterly adjustments rather than a single enormous move.
Officials expect the usual channel---dearer mortgages, auto loans, and corporate credit---to
cool spending without causing a depression.

That channel is working, but another one is pushing the other way.  Treasury must refinance
at the Fed's higher rates, and bondholders spend part of the resulting interest income.  Debt
has reached 163.40 percent of GDP and interest expense is 17.10 percent of GDP.  The bill
rate offered to buyers is 11.33 percent after the market-clearing spread.  September
begins a run of at least four consecutive quarters above 4 percent inflation.  The Fed keeps
raising rates in this history.  Its dilemma is already visible: every increase removes some
private demand now and adds to federal interest income as Treasury refinances later.

\paragraph{Thursday, June 16, 2039---the market reaches the edge.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Treasury Buyers Demand 13-Percent Bill Rates: The government expects to sell \$26.6 trillion
of securities this quarter, including repeated refinancing of short-term debt.  Dealers say
the autumn financing calendar could exceed the amount private portfolios can absorb at any
yield contemplated by current policy.
\end{quote}

Inflation is 4.89 percent, the Fed's short-rate setting is 8.72 percent, and the additional
auction-clearing spread has reached 4.63 percentage points.  New bills yield 13.35 percent.
Debt stands at 178.82 percent of GDP, interest expense at 20.49 percent of annualized GDP,
and the total deficit at 21.20 percent.  Required issuance is already 1.488 times the
reference amount under the paper's central buyer curve.

That curve fails in the next quarter.  It can absorb \$27.303 trillion, while Treasury needs
to place \$27.341 trillion, leaving \$37.4 billion without a private buyer.  The dollar
shortfall is small beside the auction, but its meaning is large: another increase in yield
has reached the curve's assumed capacity ceiling.  Borrowing the missing amount would add to
the securities requiring buyers.  A temporary Federal Reserve facility can bridge the
auction; permanent financing now requires lower primary borrowing, a larger captive buyer
base, monetary accommodation, or changed payments.

\paragraph{Thursday, September 15, 2039---Congress chooses the settlement.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
President Signs Four-Year Fiscal Accord; Fed Opens Temporary Treasury Facility: The law
combines a broad consumption levy, additional income and payroll revenue, slower growth in
retirement and health benefits, and limits on other spending.  Leaders of both parties say
the package is intended to end emergency central-bank support by restoring private demand
for federal debt.
\end{quote}

At full implementation, the law improves the primary balance by 5.48 percent of GDP relative
to the previous path.  The change arrives in sixteen equal quarterly steps, spreading a
fiscal adjustment with few peacetime U.S. precedents over four years.  The fictional law protects
low-income transfers and public investment while distributing the remainder broadly; the
simulator represents only its aggregate primary-balance effect.  Legislative scoring of the
invented components would require a separate tax-and-benefit model.

The Fed provides market liquidity while the first measures take effect, and the announced
fiscal path brings private buyers back.  New bill, note, and long-bond rates fall to 8.50,
9.00, and 9.00 percent.  Those rates are published recovery assumptions, followed by a
gradual decline rather than an overnight return to normal.  The first-quarter fiscal
improvement is only 0.34 percent of GDP.  Interest expense reaches 21.01 percent of GDP, the
total deficit 22.36 percent, and debt 182.74 percent.  The rescue has stopped the auction
seizure; years of high-coupon debt remain to be worked through.

\paragraph{Thursday, September 20, 2040---the price of stabilization.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Inflation Peaks at 5.2 Percent During Second Year of Contraction: Unemployment is rising,
households are absorbing the new tax system, and Congress has rejected proposals to suspend
the fiscal accord.  Treasury bill rates have declined to 7.5 percent.
\end{quote}

Real GDP is contracting at a 1-percent annual rate and inflation reaches the recovery path's
5.2-percent peak.  The fiscal improvement has reached 1.71 percent of GDP, enough to bring
the primary budget approximately into balance.  Interest from the inherited stock still
equals 19.67 percent of GDP, leaving a 19.61-percent total deficit and debt at 195.42 percent
of GDP.  The public sees a recession, higher taxes, slower benefit growth, and continuing
inflation at the same time.  The political bargain survives because the Fed's bridge is
temporary, payments continue, and each quarter's new debt carries a lower rate than the
stock it is beginning to replace.

\paragraph{Thursday, June 18, 2043---debt peaks after the crisis has ended.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Debt Reaches 209 Percent of GDP, Yet Treasury Yields Fall Again: Budget officials report a
3.9-percent primary surplus and say the debt ratio is at its turning point.  Inflation has
declined to 4.3 percent and real growth has recovered above 2 percent.
\end{quote}

The fiscal package is now fully phased in.  New bills yield 5.24 percent and long bonds 6.04
percent, while the inherited marketable stock still carries an average effective rate of
7.81 percent.  Interest expense is 17.24 percent of GDP.  The government therefore runs a
13.37-percent total deficit even with the large primary surplus.  Debt/GDP nevertheless
peaks at 209.27 percent: nominal output is growing fast enough, and new borrowing slowly
enough, for the denominator to begin overtaking the debt stock.  Recovery means reversing
the ratio's direction long before the nominal debt is repaid.

\paragraph{Thursday, December 19, 2047---the emergency becomes a durable compact.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Debt Ratio Falls for a Fourth Year as Inflation Recedes to 3.3 Percent: Congress renews the
core budget rules after an election fought over how to divide the continuing primary surplus.
The Federal Reserve reports that emergency Treasury support has ended.
\end{quote}

Debt has declined to 202.08 percent of GDP.  Interest expense remains enormous at 12.03
percent of GDP, but the 3.95-percent primary surplus reduces the total deficit to 8.08
percent.  Real growth is 1.94 percent, inflation is 3.34 percent, bills yield 4.56 percent,
and long bonds 5.36 percent.  The consumption levy and benefit changes have become ordinary,
unpopular features of federal policy.  Markets treat them as durable enough to finance the
remaining deficit without the emergency facility.

\paragraph{Thursday, September 14, 2056---America has recovered, with scars.}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Inflation Near 2.5 Percent as Debt Falls to Its 2039 Share of the Economy: Treasury reports
another year of declining debt/GDP and no payment disruption since the crisis.  The federal
interest bill remains close to 9 percent of GDP, preserving intense pressure on every budget.
\end{quote}

Debt ends at 178.82 percent of GDP and has fallen 2.55 percentage points during the preceding
four quarters.  Inflation is 2.5 percent, real growth 1.7 percent, new bills yield 4.3
percent, and long bonds 5.1 percent.  The average effective rate on the marketable stock has
declined to 4.68 percent as the old crisis cohorts mature.  Interest expense is still 8.88
percent of GDP.  A 4.03-percent primary surplus leaves a 4.85-percent total deficit that
nominal growth can absorb while the ratio continues downward.

Prices have risen 82 percent over the seventeen-year recovery window.  How nominal wages,
asset values, taxes, and benefits divide that burden lies beyond these equations.  The
relatively happy ending is narrow and concrete: auctions function, inflation returns near
its old range, real output grows, no Treasury payment is missed, and the debt ratio has been
turned.  America survives by replacing its former fiscal promises with a permanent compact
large enough to support the debt it accumulated.

\section{Where the crisis and recovery come from, in graphs}

The first three figures show the unresolved stress path.  They explain the pressure that
forces the 2039 settlement and show where the system goes when policy never changes.  The
fourth figure begins at the regime switch and displays the conditional recovery.  Separating
the two pieces preserves an essential distinction: the pre-settlement feedback is generated
inside the sequential model, while post-settlement growth, inflation, and marginal yields
are declared paths used to calculate the required fiscal closure and Treasury cash flows.

\subsection*{The imposed shock gives way to self-sustaining rate pressure}

calculated result.  The gold line supplies the 500-basis-point Treasury premium for ten years.

rule adds an increasing premium to long bonds.  By the time the original panic premium ends
in 2036, the debt premium is already 137 basis points; it reaches 464 basis points by 2056.

The right panel shows the rates paid on new securities.  Bills take the entire five-point
shock and the full change in the Fed's short-rate setting.  Long bonds begin from a higher
reference rate, receive the panic premium through 2036, and subsequently retain pressure from

long-run rise.

\section{The sequential fiscal--monetary loop}

The sequential experiment preserves a parallel no-shock Treasury ledger.  Only cash
interest created by the stress enters the new demand channel.  Comparing interest as a
share of scenario and reference GDP prevents a higher scenario price level from being
mistaken for additional real purchasing power.

The starting allocation of interest recipients uses approximate 2026 official aggregates.
CBO reports \$32.095 trillion of FY2026 debt held by the public and \$4.540 trillion held by
the Federal Reserve.  Treasury's June 2026 TIC table reports \$9.299 trillion of foreign
holdings \cite{tic}.  The residual allocation is 56.88 percent domestic private, 28.97
percent foreign, and 14.15 percent Federal Reserve.  The timing and valuation conventions
differ, so these aggregates serve as benchmark shares.  A security-level ownership
reconciliation lies outside this calibration.

In the central case, domestic private recipients spend 25 percent of incremental cash
interest on domestic output, foreign recipients spend 5 percent domestically, and the
Federal Reserve share contributes nothing directly to demand.  The effective fraction is
therefore
\[
m_I=(0.5688)(0.25)+(0.2897)(0.05)+(0.1415)(0)=0.1567.
\]
The domestic and foreign spending fractions remain scenario assumptions.  Research on the
redistribution and interest-rate-exposure channels supports modeling heterogeneous marginal
propensities to consume.  This experiment supplies the aggregate coefficient \cite{auclert}.
The BIS likewise notes that higher government interest payments
to private bondholders can support demand and weaken monetary transmission when public debt
is high \cite{bisDebt2026}.

Let $x_t$ be the output gap, $a_t$ the policy-rate adjustment relative to the reference path,
$d^P_t$ the automatic-stabilizer primary-deficit impulse as a share of GDP, and $d^I_t$ the
spendable incremental-interest impulse.  The central quarterly rule is
\[
x_{t+1}=0.80x_t-0.25\Delta r^{real}_{t-1}-0.15s^{private}_{t-1}
        +0.80d^P_t+1.00d^I_t.
\]
Inflation is represented as a deviation from the CBO reference path:
\[
\pi_{t+1}=\pi^0_{t+1}+0.98(\pi_t-\pi^0_t)+0.06x_{t-1}.
\]
The policy adjustment follows
\[
a_t=0.50a_{t-1}+0.50\left[1.50(\pi_t-\pi^0_t)+0.50x_t\right].
\]
The short Treasury rate receives the full policy adjustment; notes receive 75 percent and
long bonds and TIPS real rates receive 50 percent.  An output gap of $x_t$ changes the
quarterly primary deficit by $-0.45x_t$ times annualized potential nominal GDP divided by
four.  These equations make the mechanism reproducible.  Their coefficients are reduced-form
sensitivities; a fully estimated U.S. macroeconomic model remains future work.

The \href{confidence_shock_review.pdf}{companion review} also examines the

\section{Debt deterioration can sustain the rate pressure}

The initiating premium disappears after ten years.  A second experiment lets the debt path
itself affect later Treasury yields.  Bhatt, Diercks, Eyal, and Skaperdas estimate that a
one-percentage-point increase in expected U.S. debt/GDP raises the longer-run neutral rate by
about 1--2 basis points and the ten-year term premium by about 2--3 basis points
\cite{fedDebtRates}.  The model applies lower, central, and upper translations to the lagged
scenario debt/GDP gap relative to the parallel reference path.

The central translation adds 1.5 basis points per debt-ratio point to note and TIPS real
rates and 4 basis points per point to long-bond rates; bills receive no debt adjustment.  The
source estimates expected debt effects.  The published estimate anchors the scale; this
experiment supplies a one-quarter lag, a realized debt gap, and the maturity mapping.

The feedback supplies an ongoing mechanism behind the one-time confidence label.  The
initial shock creates a debt-ratio gap.  That gap raises subsequent financing rates under the model's
translation of the published sensitivity.  Higher interest enlarges debt and spendable bondholder income, producing a
larger gap in the next round.  A nonlinear jump associated with panic, currency loss, or an
auction disruption lies outside the local empirical estimate and the current model.

\section{A finite financing-capacity boundary}

Mandatory placement keeps the path alive even at high yields.  The final experiment gives
aggregate Treasury financing a finite capacity.  Let $Q_t$ be
required gross issuance and $Q^0_t$ reference issuance scaled to scenario GDP.  Required
capacity is $M_t=Q_t/Q^0_t$.  At an aggregate nominal yield gap $\Delta y_t$, supplied
capacity is
\[
K_t=\min\!\left(\bar K,\;\max\!\left[0,1+\eta
\frac{\Delta y_t}{100\text{ basis points}}\right]\right).
\]
The model iterates a common spread across issuance types until $K_t\ge M_t$.  When
$M_t>\bar K$, no supported yield can place that quarter's issuance and the run stops.

Three transparent sensitivities are reported.  The tight curve adds 2.5 percent of reference
capacity per 100 basis points and stops at 1.25 times reference issuance.  The central curve
adds 5 percent and stops at 1.5 times.  The wide curve adds 10 percent and stops at 2 times.
These curves are illustrative sensitivities, not estimated Treasury demand curves.
Gross issuance includes replacement of maturing principal; it is not net demand
for additional government liabilities. A ceiling on that turnover does not identify
national fiscal capacity or a shortage of savings. The reported failure dates
describe this supplied rule, not a discovered U.S. financing limit.
The reference normalization includes ordinary institutional absorption implicit in
the baseline; incremental Federal Reserve intervention is absent.

fiscal package, regulatory mandate, capital inflow, inflationary accommodation, or delayed
payment.  Stopping the run makes that missing choice visible.
Continuing to issue debt after the boundary would silently restore the unlimited-absorption
assumption that the experiment was designed to test.

\section{What a Federal Reserve backstop means}

Competitive Treasury bidders set the auction's stop-out yield.  Federal Reserve rules exclude
direct purchases of newly issued securities from Treasury and competitive auction bids
\cite{fedTreasuryPurchases}.  SOMA can roll over maturing holdings through noncompetitive
tenders.  Those tenders are add-ons to the announced offering and receive the stop-out yield
established by competitive bids \cite{nyFedRollovers}.

The Federal Reserve can support market functioning through repos and secondary-market
purchases, as it did during the Treasury-market disruption of March 2020.  Such intervention
can make an outright failed auction remote.  The auction yield and consolidated public-sector
cost remain.  Purchases create reserve liabilities; a price-stability regime remunerates those
reserves at the policy rate, changing the maturity of the public liability while preserving
the consolidated interest expense.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
The capacity boundary should therefore trigger explicit alternatives:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Price stability with fiscal closure.}  The Federal Reserve maintains the
inflation response while Congress changes taxes or primary spending.
\item \textbf{Sterilized market support.}  The Federal Reserve buys Treasuries for market
functioning and continues paying the shadow policy rate on reserves.
\item \textbf{Fiscal dominance or a yield cap.}  Treasury yields are suppressed while the
inflation and credibility consequences enter the macro path.
\item \textbf{Forced or subsidized absorption.}  Regulation, capital controls, guarantees,
or balance-sheet mandates expand demand and reallocate the loss.
\item \textbf{Payment disruption or restructuring.}  Contractual claims change after the
other margins prove unavailable.
\end{enumerate}
The recovery chronology selects the first alternative and uses temporary market support as
a bridge.  A separate run carries the central private-clearing path through 2039Q2 and starts
the conditional fiscal closure in 2039Q3.  The assumed fall in marginal yields represents
the combined effect of the facility and the enacted package; Federal Reserve purchases and
reserve creation are outside that recovery ledger.  A future endogenous regime switch can
replace those declared post-settlement paths with policy and investor behavior.

The success condition deliberately determines the fiscal number.  Post-settlement growth,
inflation, and yields are scenario choices.  The probability that Congress can enact the
package and investors will reward it with the assumed rates remains unestimated.  The
result is a conditional existence proof grounded in the actual inherited coupon structure:
America can recover from the 2039 state under a package of this scale, while the recovery
takes seventeen years and leaves interest expense far above its pre-crisis share.

The model omits endogenous expectations, exchange rates, capital flight, bank and dealer

default dynamics, and general equilibrium.  Crisis probability remains outside the model.  Its 99-
percent rate cap is a numerical boundary; the extreme full-spending path reaches it and is
reported only as a stress failure.

These omissions cut in different directions.  Fiscal action or a credible monetary-fiscal
package could reduce the premium and stop the loop.  Expectations, currency depreciation,
forced selling, or a shrinking tax base could accelerate it.  The reported dates should move
when the interest-spending and financing-capacity parameters are estimated more carefully.

\section{Conclusion}

The model develops one conditional answer to the project's original question in four steps.  Treasury accounting can
carry debt forward indefinitely whenever every auction is guaranteed to clear.  The
zero-spending control demonstrates that property: debt reaches 252 percent of GDP while
inflation stays near 2 percent.  An accounting ledger therefore cannot discover a national
breaking point by itself.

Economic feedback supplies the pressure missing from that ledger.  At the central
15.67-percent spendable-interest share, inflation remains above 4 percent beginning in
2037Q3 and reaches 8.04 percent in 2056.  Debt-dependent yields intensify the loop: the
unchanged-policy path reaches 11.26 percent inflation and 290 percent debt/GDP.  Every tested
Fed response at the central spending share leaves inflation pressure rising because higher
rates eventually enlarge Treasury's interest transfer enough to work against private-demand
restraint.

The imposed buyer-capacity limit turns that pressure into a model event. The central
curve reaches its limit in 2039Q3 when debt-dependent yields are active; tight and wide
curves move the event from 2034Q3 to beyond 2056. At the boundary, automatic private
placement is no longer permitted by the chosen rule. This does not establish when
actual investors would refuse financing; that requires evidence beyond the assumed
gross-issuance ceiling.

The conditional recovery supplies the final step.  Washington bridges the 2039 auction,
enacts a permanent 5.48-percent-of-GDP primary-balance improvement over four years, and
is assumed to receive a gradual normalization of marginal rates.  Inflation peaks at 5.2 percent and the economy
contracts for a year.  Debt keeps rising to 209 percent of GDP in 2043 as inherited coupons
work through the budget, then falls to 179 percent in 2056 with inflation at 2.5 percent.
Interest expense remains 8.9 percent of GDP, so the recovered country still operates under a
much tighter and more contentious fiscal compact.  Rate normalization without the fiscal
package leaves debt at 265 percent of GDP under the same favorable macro assumptions.

America survives this history because the regime changes before double-digit inflation and
near-20-percent policy rates arrive.  The adjustment redistributes resources on a historic
scale, leaves a higher price level and an unprecedented debt stock, and permanently narrows
the federal government's choices.  It also preserves functioning auctions, positive real
growth, declining inflation, and an improving debt ratio.  That relatively happy ending is
available under the stated assumptions; the model does not estimate its likelihood
or identify the incremental cost of delaying a comparable settlement.
